*Adults and adolescents aged ≥12 years · Australian Asthma Handbook v3.0 framework · Evidence checked 22 October 2026*

## 1. Why this topic matters

An acute asthma question tests more than bronchodilator recall. The central decisions are whether the patient is tiring, which treatment must start immediately, and when further escalation or admission is safer than discharge. A quieter chest is not necessarily a better chest.

Australia’s asthma burden remains substantial:

| Indicator | Verified Australian estimate |
|---|---|
| People living with asthma | Around 2.8 million; 11% of the population, 2022 |
| Deaths attributed to asthma | 478 in 2024 |
| Health-system expenditure | Approximately A$1.3 billion, 2023–24 |
| Hospital-services expenditure | A$427.5 million, 2023–24 |
| Share of total disease burden | 2.5% of DALYs, 2024 |
| ED presentations, principal diagnosis asthma | Around 60,300, 2024–25 |

These figures cover asthma across all ages, not adult exacerbations alone. Expenditure is not the economic cost of acute attacks, and hospital services include more than inpatient admissions. The mortality update comes from the National Asthma Council’s ABS-commissioned 2024 data; the AIHW page still reports 480 deaths for 2023. [AIHW](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/chronic-respiratory-conditions/asthma); [NAC mortality data](https://files.nationalasthma.org.au/resources/Asthma-Mortality-Data-2024-Final.pdf).

## 2. First look: recognise danger before ordering tests

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, speech, effort, consciousness and room-air SpO₂ while treatment is prepared. Choose the most serious category supported by the findings; do not average reassuring and dangerous features.

| Immediate ED category | Key findings |
|---|---|
| Mild–moderate | Can walk and speak full sentences in one breath; SpO₂ >94% |
| Severe: any feature | Cannot finish sentences; accessory-muscle recruitment or obvious distress; SpO₂ ≤94% |
| Life-threatening: any feature | Confusion, agitation, exhaustion, collapse or reduced consciousness; cyanosis; poor effort or soft/absent breath sounds; SpO₂ <92% |

This is the Handbook’s **immediate assessment**, not a universal classification. Its separate secondary-assessment table uses SpO₂ >96%, 92–96% and <92% for mild–moderate, severe and life-threatening categories respectively. An MCQ specifying the assessment stage therefore matters. [Immediate treatment table](https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au/pdf/6063); [secondary assessment table](https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au/pdf/6069).

Also distinguish a severe **attack** from chronic **severe asthma**. The former describes today’s emergency; the latter describes the underlying condition and treatment requirements. A patient without a severe-asthma label can still have a life-threatening attack. [Handbook definitions](https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au/asthma-exacerbations-and-acute-asthma-definitions-and-classification).

## 3. Start inhaled treatment promptly

For an Australian Handbook-based question, use the following initial doses. A pMDI plus spacer is appropriate when the patient can use it; severe disease does not automatically require a nebuliser.

| Presentation | Salbutamol | Ipratropium |
|---|---|---|
| Mild–moderate | 4–12 actuations, 100 micrograms each, via pMDI/spacer | Not routine initially |
| Severe | 12 actuations via pMDI/spacer; if unable to use spacer, nebulise 5 mg | 8 actuations, 21 micrograms each, via pMDI/spacer |
| Life-threatening | Two 5 mg nebules via continuous oxygen-driven nebulisation, with immediate higher-level care | 500 micrograms added to nebulised solution |

Repeat salbutamol every 20 minutes during the first hour according to response, sooner if required. Repeat ipratropium every 20 minutes during that hour for severe/life-threatening attacks; the nebulised alternative is 500 micrograms. Reassess rather than administering repeated doses mechanically. [Handbook immediate treatment](https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au/pdf/6063).

**Technique is treatment:** shake before each actuation, give one actuation at a time, and allow tidal breathing through the spacer when too breathless for a single-breath technique. Do not load multiple actuations into the chamber together. [NAC spacer instructions](https://www.nationalasthma.org.au/living-with-asthma/how-to-videos/how-to-use-a-puffer-with-a-spacer-for-adults).

## 4. Oxygen: treat hypoxaemia, not breathlessness alone

For adults without a hypercapnic-respiratory-failure risk, start supplemental oxygen when room-air SpO₂ is **<92%**, targeting **92–96%**. The target is **88–92%** when an established risk of hypercapnia calls for controlled oxygen, for example relevant chronic respiratory disease. Do not assign the lower target simply because the patient has asthma. Check blood gases when clinically indicated. [TSANZ oxygen position statement](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/resp.14218).

The assessment threshold and oxygen threshold answer different questions: an SpO₂ of 93% can indicate a severe presentation without requiring oxygen solely on that number. Continue clinical assessment; oxygen saturation does not measure ventilation. Likewise, a satisfactory saturation after oxygen cannot establish that an exhausted patient is safe.

## 5. Corticosteroids and magnesium: remember the population

Give systemic corticosteroids within **one hour** when indicated, including all severe/life-threatening attacks. The Handbook specifies:

- **Adults:** oral prednisolone/prednisone **37.5–50 mg daily for 5–10 days**.
- **Adolescents:** **1 mg/kg, maximum 50 mg daily, for 3–5 days**.

For an inadequate response, consider IV magnesium sulfate: **0.2 mmol/kg, maximum 10 mmol, over 20 minutes**. It supplements—not replaces—inhaled treatment. [Handbook ED recommendations](https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au/acute-asthma/adults-and-adolescents/managing-acute-asthma-in-adults-and-adolescents-in-the-ed).

Do not claim that magnesium benefits every exacerbation. An August 2026 umbrella review supports IV magnesium as a secondary adjunct in refractory severe disease, with methodological limitations across the underlying evidence; it does not justify routine magnesium for mild attacks or routine nebulised magnesium. [Wang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2026.1917060).

## 6. Reassessment: look for fatigue and treatment toxicity

Once feasible, complete objective assessment without delaying emergency care. The Handbook’s secondary table identifies severe findings including respiratory rate **≥25/min**, heart rate **≥110/min**, and FEV₁ **≤50% predicted or personal best**. Life-threatening findings include reduced air entry, bradypnoea, bradycardia or arrhythmia, and inability to perform spirometry.

Blood-gas danger signals include PaO₂ **<60 mmHg**, PaCO₂ **>50 mmHg**, a normal PaCO₂ despite low PaO₂, or pH **<7.35**. These are assessment findings, not automatic intubation cut-offs. A “normal” CO₂ is not reassuring in a hypoxaemic, struggling patient. [Secondary assessment](https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au/pdf/6069).

Persistent tachypnoea also needs interpretation. High-dose salbutamol may produce hypokalaemia and lactic acidosis with compensatory hyperventilation. Compare airflow, effort, blood gases and treatment exposure before assuming every rapid breath requires more salbutamol. [GINA 2026, p.20](https://ginasthma.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/GINA-2026-Strategy-Report-WMS.pdf).

## 7. Escalation: act on the trajectory

Life-threatening features warrant immediate senior ED and ICU/anaesthetic involvement while bronchodilators and other indicated treatment continue. Progressive exhaustion, impaired consciousness, deteriorating ventilation or refractory hypoxaemia demand an airway plan; do not wait for a memorised PaCO₂ threshold.

Intubation and ventilation in severe asthma require experienced clinicians because air trapping and dynamic hyperinflation complicate ventilation. A contemporary ICU review emphasises timely escalation and strategies limiting hyperinflation; rescue VV-ECMO is an expert-centre option when conventional management fails, not the next routine ED step. [Abedi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/08850666261466238).

NIV is not established routine treatment for all acute asthma. A 2025 systematic review included **8 randomised trials and 5 observational studies**. Its randomised-trial estimate for intubation was **RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.16–1.29**: the interval crosses 1, and evidence certainty was low to very low. Selected patients may benefit from a closely supervised trial, but this is not proof that NIV reliably avoids intubation. [Homer-Bouthiette and Wilson, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39642363/).

## 8. Discharge is an active treatment decision

The Handbook recommends observation for **at least four hours after distress has resolved and symptoms have stabilised**, repeat spirometry before discharge, GP review **within three days**, and comprehensive reassessment in **2–4 weeks**. [Handbook ED recommendations](https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au/acute-asthma/adults-and-adolescents/managing-acute-asthma-in-adults-and-adolescents-in-the-ed).

Do not discharge on salbutamol alone. The discharge table supports adequate ICS-containing maintenance treatment, including MART where appropriate. One short-term post-exacerbation option is **budesonide–formoterol DPI 200/6 micrograms: two inhalations twice daily, plus one as needed; maximum 12 total inhalations/day**, including maintenance. This is formulation-specific: do not transfer the puff count to another strength or device. Beclometasone–formoterol in this table is restricted to adults **≥18 years**. [Handbook discharge table](https://www.asthmahandbook.org.au/pdf/6071).

Practical discharge reasoning also asks whether the patient understands the regimen, can use the device, has the medicines and action plan, and can access review or return if deterioration occurs. A transient bronchodilator response is not the whole discharge assessment.

## 9. What has changed since the original draft?

The Handbook remains the Australian anchor, but **GINA 2026** is newer than its cited GINA 2025 evidence. GINA now uses an oxygen target of **92–95%** and more conservative SABA dosing; its ED figure gives **6–10 salbutamol puffs** for severe presentations and allows ICS–formoterol for mild presentations. These are **GINA-specific**, not replacements silently inserted into the Handbook table. [GINA 2026, pp.20 and 186](https://ginasthma.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/GINA-2026-Strategy-Report-WMS.pdf).

For MCQs, identify the named guideline and setting first. “Newer” does not mean every number can be mixed into an older algorithm.

## 10. Three quick MCQ checks

**Worked example — fictional teaching vignette:** An adult can speak only a few words, has room-air SpO₂ 93%, and is using accessory muscles. The immediate Handbook category is severe. The appropriate reasoning is to start the severe-attack treatment bundle and reassess, rather than dismiss the episode because oxygen saturation remains above the usual oxygen-start threshold. If the next observation describes exhaustion and declining effort, the category changes: the new life-threatening findings outweigh the earlier saturation. This illustrates why a sequence of observations can matter more than a single isolated number. It also explains why the best answer may change when an MCQ adds one sentence about mental state or respiratory effort.

**1. A patient is confused, exhausted and barely wheezing. Is the quieter chest reassuring?** No. The pattern is life-threatening; initiate treatment and escalation together. Do not wait for lung-function testing to confirm danger.

**2. A markedly breathless, hypoxaemic patient has a normal PaCO₂. Does this exclude ventilatory failure?** No. Interpret the result alongside effort, mental state and trajectory. A laboratory reference interval is not a safety certificate.

**3. A question explicitly cites the Australian Handbook v3.0. Should you replace its salbutamol and oxygen figures with GINA 2026 values?** No. Answer within the specified framework, while recognising the newer international update.

**Take-home:** recognise the worst feature, deliver inhaled treatment effectively, reassess the response, escalate before exhaustion becomes arrest, and prescribe prevention before discharge.
